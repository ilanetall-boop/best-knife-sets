What's actually in the box

What's in the 18-piece block

Eighteen is the largest headline piece count of any set we've reviewed on this site, bigger than the Ninja NeverDull's 17 and well past the Henckels Statement's 15. What Chicago Cutlery's listing title does not do is itemize those 18 pieces the way some of the budget sharpener blocks in this catalogue do — there's no bullet-by-bullet breakdown of exactly how many are chef's knives, utility knives, steak knives or the block and sharpener counted as pieces in their own right. The title commits to a total; it doesn't commit to a list.

That matters because, as we've seen elsewhere in this catalogue, a "15-piece" set can mean seven knives and a shear, or eight knives and six steak knives, depending entirely on how the manufacturer counts. There is no reason to assume an 18-piece Chicago Cutlery block splits any differently, and we won't guess at a breakdown the listing doesn't give us. If the exact mix of knives versus steak knives versus block hardware matters to your decision, the listing's own bullet points and product photos are the place to check it, not an assumption carried over from a smaller set elsewhere on this page.

What is explicit in the title is the block material: wood, rather than the unspecified block material on sets like the Ninja NeverDull. A wooden block is also, by construction, bulkier on a counter than a slim in-drawer tray, and at 18 pieces this is not a small object. The listing doesn't publish its footprint, so we won't estimate one here — our guide to measuring a knife block before you buy walks through how to check a block's real footprint from listing photos before it arrives on your counter.

What's in your hand

Triple-riveted poly handles: what they're actually made of

The listing title specifies "Triple Rivet Poly" handles, finished in black and described as ergonomic. "Poly" is shorthand for a polymer handle material — a molded synthetic rather than wood, bone or a cast metal bolster — and triple rivet describes the fastening, not the material: three visible rivets running through the handle where it meets the blade's tang, the same construction style used on several of the Henckels sets we've compared and on Cuisinart's forged line elsewhere in this catalogue. Three rivets through a polymer handle is a genuinely sturdy, dishwasher-resistant way to build a handle; it is also a different material story from the solid wood or stainless-steel-and-rivet handles found on some premium sets.

What the listing does not state is how far the steel runs inside that polymer handle. Triple rivets are often, though not always, a sign that the tang runs the handle's full length — there generally isn't much structural point in triple-riveting a handle onto a short stub of steel. But "often" is not "confirmed," and Chicago Cutlery's listing never uses the words "full tang" the way some competing listings do explicitly. If a confirmed full-tang claim is the deciding factor for you, that's a detail to verify on the current listing photos rather than infer from the rivet count alone, the same caution worth applying to any set in this price range that doesn't spell the claim out in writing.

The practical read on "poly": it is a durable, grippable, and typically lighter handle material than wood or metal, and it's the same broad category used across a wide range of block sets at every price point in this catalogue. It is not, on its own, a sign of either a premium or a budget build — it's simply what the listing tells us is in your hand, stated plainly rather than described in marketing language that implies more than it confirms.

The feature every tier shares

The built-in sharpener, against the cheaper blocks

Chicago Cutlery's built-in sharpener sits in the same functional category as the sharpening slots we've covered at every price point in this catalogue: a fixed mechanism set into the block itself, so the knife picks up a touch-up pass simply by being put away. That's mechanically the same basic idea behind the Ninja NeverDull's sharpening slot at a similar price, and behind KitchenAid's Gourmet 15-piece set at barely a third of this price. The mechanism's core job — removing a small, consistent amount of steel on the way back into the block — doesn't change much from one listing to the next. What changes is what each manufacturer is willing to publish about it.

On that measure, Chicago Cutlery's listing is no more specific than the budget tier: no stated sharpening angle, no named abrasive material, no pass-count rating before the mechanism wears. That puts it on equal published footing with KitchenAid's $75.98 set and the sharpener-equipped McCook and Astercook sets we've compared elsewhere, none of which publish those details either. If a built-in sharpener is the entire reason you're shopping in this category, the mechanism here is not demonstrably better, on the facts each listing actually states, than one built into a block costing a third of the price.

What Chicago Cutlery's price is buying alongside the sharpener, then, is the wooden block itself, the larger 18-piece count, and the triple-riveted handle construction — not a more capable or more precisely specified sharpening slot. That's worth separating clearly before the $165.82 figure gets credited entirely to the one feature every tier in this catalogue already includes.

The name on the block

A heritage brand name: does it change the steel?

Chicago Cutlery is one of the older names in American kitchen cutlery, with a history that predates most of the direct-to-consumer knife brands sold on Amazon today, including the appliance-crossover names like KitchenAid that have only recently entered the knife-block category. That kind of longevity is a real, if narrow, form of credibility: a brand that has been making knives in the US for decades generally has an established customer-service and replacement-parts infrastructure behind it, the same practical advantage we've written about separately when looking at what knife-set warranties actually cover.

What a long-standing name does not automatically do is change the steel in the box you're buying today. Chicago Cutlery's listing title describes the blades only as "Sharp Stainless Steel" — it does not state a steel grade, a country of origin for the steel, a Rockwell hardness figure, or whether the blades are stamped or forged. That's a meaningfully less specific construction claim than Henckels' stamped, German-engineered Statement line or the forged, imported-steel claim on KitchenAid's Gourmet set. A heritage name is a statement about the company's history, not a substitute for a published steel spec, and we won't credit this listing with a construction claim it doesn't make just because the brand itself is an old one.

The honest framing: buy into Chicago Cutlery's history if a long-running American name and its customer-support track record matter to you. Don't buy into it expecting that history to have quietly upgraded the stainless steel beyond what the listing actually states, because nothing in the published listing says it has.

The number on the page

Where $165.82 lands against the Henckels and Ninja tiers

At $165.82, this set sits almost exactly where the Ninja NeverDull 17-Piece Block Set does at $229.99 — within $9 of each other, one piece apart, both with a sharpener built into the block. The practical difference between the two at that shared price point is what each listing is willing to state: Ninja's publishes a German stainless steel claim and an optimized edge angle for its blades, while Chicago Cutlery's stops at "Sharp Stainless Steel" without a country of origin, a grade, or an angle. Paying essentially the same money, you're getting one more piece from Chicago Cutlery and one more published construction detail from Ninja.

Against the Henckels Statement 15-Piece set at $118.73, Chicago Cutlery's $165.82 is a little under double the price, for three more pieces, a wooden block instead of none, and a built-in sharpener Henckels' Statement line doesn't include at that tier. Henckels, in turn, states its blades are stamped and German-engineered, a specific construction claim Chicago Cutlery's listing doesn't match at any price. The two sets aren't really competing on the same claim at all: Henckels is selling engineering heritage and a stated construction method at the lower price; Chicago Cutlery is selling piece count, a wooden block and a sharpener at the higher one.

Prices on all three listings move, sometimes by a few dollars and sometimes by more. The figures above are current price-check snapshots, not guarantees, and the sensible habit before buying any of the three is the same one that applies network-wide: confirm the live Amazon price before you check out, rather than treating any number printed in a review, including this one, as fixed.

The decision

Who should buy this instead of a 15-piece set at half the price

Picture the alternative as Henckels' 15-piece Statement set at $118.73 — a little over half of Chicago Cutlery's $165.82, with a stated stamped, German-engineered construction but no built-in sharpener and three fewer pieces. Choosing the Chicago Cutlery Insignia over that cheaper alternative makes sense if you specifically want a sharpener built into the block, if a wooden block matters to you more than an unspecified or no-block alternative, if the extra pieces genuinely get used in your kitchen rather than sitting untouched, and if you'd rather buy from a long-standing American cutlery name than a German engineering claim backed by a specific stamped-blade spec.

It makes far less sense if what you actually want is a checkable steel claim to hold the purchase to. Henckels' sheet states its blades are stamped; Chicago Cutlery's doesn't commit to stamped or forged at all. If a published construction method is the deciding factor, the cheaper Henckels set gives you more to verify for less money, even without a sharpener in the block. And if your honest habit is running a knife through a sharpening slot a few times a week and nothing more demanding, that mechanism performs the same basic job here as it does on a $75.98 KitchenAid block — which means a meaningful share of the extra $145 over Henckels is paying for piece count, the wooden block and the name, not a more capable edge or a sharper mechanism.

The bottom line

The verdict

At $165.82, the Chicago Cutlery Insignia 18-Piece set is a large, triple-riveted, wooden-block set with a genuine built-in sharpener from one of the older names in American cutlery — and a listing that's notably light on checkable steel specifics for the price. It beats a cheaper 15-piece set on piece count, on having a built-in sharpener at all, and on a dedicated wooden block. It does not beat a cheaper set on anything the listing could actually prove about the steel itself, because the listing doesn't make that claim in the first place.

Buy this set if the piece count, the wooden block and a long-standing American name genuinely matter to you, and $165.82 doesn't move your budget. Buy Henckels' 15-piece Statement set instead if a stated, checkable construction method matters more than a sharpener and a bigger count, or the Ninja NeverDull instead if you want a similarly priced sharpener block that publishes more about its own steel. On the facts each listing actually states, Chicago Cutlery's premium over the Henckels tier is paying for pieces, wood and a name — not for a steel spec it never puts in writing.
