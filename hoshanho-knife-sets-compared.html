Comparison · One Brand, Three Tiers

The Cheapest Set Has the Hardest Steel.

HOSHANHO sells three knife sets, and the price climbs the way the piece count suggests it should: $146.99 for five pieces, $189.99 for seven, $208.96 for sixteen — a $61.97 spread from smallest to largest. What doesn’t climb with the price is hardness. The $146.99 five-piece set states a 62 HRC core, the hardest number anywhere in this lineup. The $208.96 sixteen-piece set, nearly $62 more and eleven pieces larger, states 58 HRC. We read all three listings line by line to work out what that four-point gap, and the different edge angles beneath it, actually mean before you buy by piece count alone.

The three listings

Same brand, the same core alloy family, three different hardness figures

All three HOSHANHO sets name a steel core from the same family — a high-carbon stainless alloy HOSHANHO markets as “Japanese Super Steel” or “Japanese High Carbon Stainless Steel” depending on the listing. The five-piece and sixteen-piece listings both spell the grade 10Cr15CoMoV; the seven-piece listing spells it 10Cr15MoV, one letter short. That could be a genuinely different alloy designation or inconsistent listing copy, which is common in this category — we take each listing at its own word rather than assume the two are identical or different based on one unexplained letter.

What's unambiguous is the hardness HOSHANHO chose to publish, or not publish, for each set. The $146.99 five-piece states 62 HRC. The $208.96 sixteen-piece states 58 HRC. The $189.99 seven-piece, sitting in the middle on price, states no Rockwell figure at all — it leads instead with a magnetic block and a specific double-bevel edge angle. Only two of the three numbers are directly comparable.

What's shared across the line

Forged construction and a named core alloy

Every listing describes a forged or hand-forged high-carbon stainless core, full knife rosters built around an 8-inch chef's knife, and the same general marketing language — "ultra sharp," "Japanese"-styled branding, ergonomic handles. None of the three publish a star rating or review count in the product data we hold, so we're not citing owner sentiment for any of them.

What actually changes

Piece count, hardness, edge angle and handle material

Five pieces at 62 HRC and a 15-degree edge with an olive wood handle; seven pieces at an unstated hardness with a 12-degree double-bevel edge and a rosewood handle; sixteen pieces at 58 HRC and a 15-degree edge with extras a block-only set doesn't carry. The price moves with the piece count. The hardness does not.

The entry price, the hardest core

The $146.99 five-piece: fewest knives, 66-layer Damascus, and 62 HRC

The HOSHANHO Damascus Kitchen Knife Set, 5PCS is the cheapest of the three listings at $146.99, and it's also the smallest: an 8-inch chef's knife, an 8-inch serrated bread knife, a 7-inch santoku, a 6-inch utility knife and a 3.75-inch paring knife. No block extras, no steak knives, no sharpener, no shears — five knives and nothing else, finished with an ergonomic olive wood handle and sold partly as a housewarming gift.

The steel claim is the most specific of the three listings. HOSHANHO states a 10Cr15CoMoV core built up through 66 layers of Damascus-pattern steel, hand-forged with a 15-degree edge and finished through what the listing calls triple-step water grinding to a mirror polish. It also states the hardness directly: 62 HRC, after what the listing describes as hand-forged grinding and heat treatment. That figure is the highest Rockwell number published anywhere in this three-set lineup — on the cheapest, smallest set of the three. For the mechanics of how a layered pattern like this is actually built, rather than laser-etched onto a plain blade, our guide to how Damascus steel knife sets actually work covers the welding and folding process in more depth than fits here.

The listing doesn't itemize anything beyond those five knife shapes and the handle material, so we won't guess at extras it doesn't claim. What it claims plainly is a hard, narrow-ground edge in the smallest package HOSHANHO sells under this name — the set to consider if the knives themselves, not the box around them, are what you're paying for.

The middle tier

The $189.99 seven-piece: a magnetic block, a 12-degree double bevel, and a hardness figure HOSHANHO doesn't publish

The HOSHANHO 7 Pcs Knife Set costs $189.99 — $43 more than the five-piece set — for an 8-inch chef's knife, an 8-inch bread knife, a 7-inch santoku, a 7-inch nakiri, a 6-inch utility knife, a 3.75-inch paring knife, and a magnetic knife block with a built-in sharpener, which HOSHANHO counts as the seventh piece. That's six knife shapes rather than the five-piece set's five, plus a block that both displays and resharpens the blades every time they're put away, a convenience we've covered in more depth in our look at how a built-in sharpener changes the calculation across a brand's own lineup.

The steel here is described differently than the five-piece set's. HOSHANHO states a 10Cr15MoV high-carbon steel core built through 9-layer composite forging, with vacuum heat treatment and repeated folding and hammering for toughness and chip resistance — fewer stated layers than the five-piece's 66, and no Damascus-pattern claim attached to this listing. What it states instead is the edge geometry: a precise double-sided hand-sharpened process with a 12-degree edge angle on both sides, which HOSHANHO markets as enabling cleaner slicing without crushing food fibers. A 12-degree per-side angle is narrower than the 15-degree figure stated on the five- and sixteen-piece sets, which generally means a thinner, more aggressive edge — it bites more easily into food and needs more careful handling around bones and frozen surfaces.

What this listing does not state, anywhere in the published copy we have on file, is a Rockwell hardness figure. That's a real gap, not a minor one: it means the one number that would let a buyer directly compare this set's edge retention against the five-piece's stated 62 HRC or the sixteen-piece's stated 58 HRC simply isn't published. We won't estimate one on HOSHANHO's behalf. If hardness specifically matters to your decision between these three sets, that is the detail to confirm on the current Amazon listing or in the product images before buying this one.

The largest set, the softest core

The $208.96 sixteen-piece: steak knives, shears, a sharpener — and 58 HRC

The HOSHANHO 16PCS Knife Set costs $208.96, the most expensive of the three and $18.97 above the seven-piece set. The listing itemizes its sixteen pieces precisely: an 8-inch chef's knife, an 8-inch bread knife, an 8-inch carving knife, a 7-inch santoku, a 7-inch fillet knife, a 6-inch utility knife, a 3.75-inch paring knife, six 4.5-inch steak knives, a knife sharpener and a pair of kitchen shears, all stored in a knife block. That's seven distinct knife shapes plus six steak knives plus the sharpener and shears — by far the broadest single roster of the three sets, and the only one of the three that includes steak knives or shears at all.

The steel claim mirrors the five-piece set's alloy family — a 10Cr15CoMoV Japanese stainless core — and states the same 15-degree sharpening angle. What differs is the published hardness: 58 HRC after high-temperature forging, four points below the five-piece set's stated 62 HRC. Rockwell hardness isn't a small-print afterthought in a knife listing; it's the number that determines how the edge behaves in daily use, which is exactly why the next section is worth reading before assuming the biggest, priciest set in a lineup is automatically the best-built one. For the mechanics of what a Rockwell number actually measures, our explainer on Rockwell hardness in kitchen knives goes into more depth than fits in a single comparison.

None of this makes the sixteen-piece set a worse purchase on its own terms — it's the only one of the three built for a household that wants steak knives and shears included, and the per-piece math below is dramatically in its favor. It does mean that "biggest set, highest price" and "hardest steel" point in opposite directions inside this one brand's own lineup. HOSHANHO also sells a still-larger twenty-piece set; our separate review of the HOSHANHO 20-piece set covers that top tier.

The number that matters

62 HRC against 58 HRC: what a four-point Rockwell gap actually trades

Rockwell hardness measures how much a material resists a standardized indentation test; on a kitchen knife, it's the single published figure that correlates most directly with how the edge behaves over months of use. Harder steel, generally, holds a sharp edge longer between sharpenings — there's simply more resistance to the edge rolling or deforming under normal cutting pressure. The trade is that harder steel is also more brittle: it's more prone to small chips when the edge meets something it shouldn't, a bone, a frozen surface, a glass cutting board, and it takes more time and a coarser stone to bring back once it does dull or chip.

Softer steel makes the opposite trade. The $208.96 sixteen-piece set's stated 58 HRC will likely need touch-ups sooner than the five-piece set's 62 HRC under identical use, but it's more forgiving of the occasional rough treatment a set that includes steak knives is more likely to see — steak knives regularly meet plates and bone in a way a dedicated chef's knife doesn't. A four-point Rockwell gap is a real difference in this category, not a rounding error.

The honest complication is the $189.99 seven-piece set, which publishes neither number. Its narrower 12-degree double-bevel edge is a separate spec from hardness — a knife can be ground at a fine angle on soft steel or a wide angle on hard steel, and the angle alone doesn't tell you which. Without a stated Rockwell figure, a buyer choosing the seven-piece set is trading a known edge geometry for an unknown edge-retention number, which is a different kind of uncertainty than choosing between the two sets that do publish hardness.

The math

The per-piece math: the sixteen-piece set is the cheapest piece on this page by a wide margin

Divide each price by its stated piece count and the order flips from how the total prices read. The five-piece set works out to $29.40 per piece ($146.99 ÷ 5). The seven-piece set, counting the magnetic block as the seventh piece the way HOSHANHO's own listing does, works out to about $27.14 per piece ($189.99 ÷ 7). The sixteen-piece set works out to roughly $13.06 per piece ($208.96 ÷ 16) — less than half the per-piece cost of either smaller set, because six of its sixteen pieces are steak knives rather than full kitchen knives, and two more are a sharpener and a pair of shears rather than blades at all.

That comparison only tells you something useful once you decide what you're counting. If the measure is dollars per distinct knife shape, the five-piece set's $29.40 buys five different knives for five different jobs, while a meaningful share of the sixteen-piece set's low per-piece price comes from six nearly identical steak knives. If the measure is dollars per item in the box, the sixteen-piece set wins by a wide margin. Either way, the per-piece number on its own doesn't account for hardness. A buyer optimizing purely for cost per piece who picks the sixteen-piece set is also, whether they intend to or not, choosing the softest published steel of the three.

FAQ

Common questions

Is the cheaper HOSHANHO set really harder than the pricier one?

Yes, according to HOSHANHO's own published figures. The $146.99 five-piece set states 62 HRC. The $208.96 sixteen-piece set states 58 HRC, four points lower, despite costing nearly $62 more and including eleven additional pieces. The $189.99 seven-piece set in between publishes no Rockwell figure at all, so it can't be placed on that same scale.

What's the real difference between the five-piece and sixteen-piece sets?

Both name the same 10Cr15CoMoV steel family and the same 15-degree edge angle. The sixteen-piece set adds eleven pieces the five-piece set doesn't have — two extra full-size knives, six steak knives, a sharpener and a pair of shears — and costs $61.97 more. In exchange, its stated hardness is 58 HRC against the five-piece set's 62 HRC. More pieces and a lower price per piece, for a softer published core.

Does the seven-piece set's narrower edge mean it's sharper than the others?

A narrower edge angle, 12 degrees per side here against 15 degrees on the other two sets, generally describes a thinner, more aggressive geometry that bites into food more easily and needs more careful handling around bones and frozen surfaces. That's a separate spec from hardness, though. The seven-piece listing doesn't publish a Rockwell figure, so its edge-retention behavior over time isn't directly comparable to the two sets that do state one.

Which HOSHANHO set should I actually buy?

Match it to what you're optimizing for. If hardness and edge retention matter most, the $146.99 five-piece set has the highest stated Rockwell figure of the three. If a magnetic display block with a built-in sharpener and the narrowest edge angle appeal to you, the $189.99 seven-piece set offers both, with no published hardness to weigh against them. If you want steak knives, shears and the lowest price per piece in one box, the $208.96 sixteen-piece set delivers that, with a softer stated core than the five-piece set.
