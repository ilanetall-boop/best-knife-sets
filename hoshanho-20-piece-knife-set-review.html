What's actually in the box

What “20 pieces” actually means here

The listing title commits to a headline number — 20 pieces — and a block to hold them, which is six more pieces than the 14-piece imarku gyutou set we reviewed separately and five more than the HENCKELS 15-piece set we feature as our overall top pick. What the listing does not do, in the published copy we have on file for this exact ASIN, is itemize that count knife by knife the way Cuisinart's listings typically do — an 8-inch chef, a slicer, a santoku, named one by one. HOSHANHO's copy leans on the total and the Japanese-style framing instead.

That distinction matters more here than on a cheaper set, because the gap between 14 pieces and 20 pieces is exactly the kind of gap that's usually filled by steak knives, shears and sharpening slots rather than additional knives you'll use weekly. We won't invent a piece-by-piece manifest the manufacturer hasn't published for this ASIN. If knowing precisely which six pieces separate this set from a 14-piece competitor matters to your decision — whether they're genuinely useful knife shapes or duplicate steak knives and guards — confirm it on the current Amazon listing images before you buy. What we can confirm from the title alone: 20 total pieces, a Japanese-style framing, and a block sized to match, all for $569.99.

The honest read on piece count generally, which applies here as much as anywhere in this catalogue: most home cooks reach for three knives — a chef's knife, a paring knife, a serrated bread knife — and everything past that is either an occasional-use knife or packaging. A 20-piece set does not mean twenty knives you will use. It means a bigger box, and in this case, a much bigger price tag attached to that box.

The edge claim

“Ultra sharp” and Japanese-style, at $570

The listing markets this set as ultra sharp and Japanese-style, both of which describe real, meaningful things in this category when a manufacturer backs them with a number. A genuinely Japanese-style edge is typically ground narrower than a Western double-bevel — our review of the imarku gyutou set covers a published figure of 15 degrees per side, against roughly 20 degrees on a German-style knife — and that narrower angle is what makes a blade feel sharper straight out of the box: less steel meets the food at the cutting line, so less force is needed to start a cut.

What we don't have, in the published data we hold for this specific ASIN, is a stated edge angle or a Rockwell hardness figure of the kind imarku publishes for its $169.99 set. “Ultra sharp” and “Japanese-style” are the kind of adjectives that describe a real category of knife-making, but without a specific degree figure or a hardness number attached, they are not independently checkable the way a stated 15-degree edge or a 56 ± 2 Rockwell figure is. We're not saying the edge isn't sharp — new knives of nearly any construction cut well out of the box — we're saying this listing doesn't give us a number to hold it to, and at $570 that is a more conspicuous absence than it would be on a $30 set.

For more on what a Rockwell figure actually tells you about how a blade will perform over time, and why its presence or absence on a listing is worth noticing, see our explainer on Rockwell hardness.

The hardware

The block itself: what the extra size and finish buy

A 20-piece set needs a correspondingly larger block, and that is one place where the extra money is visibly going: more slots, a bigger footprint on the counter, and in most listings at this tier, a heavier wood finish than the pinewood or basic hardwood used on entry sets. A block is a piece of furniture as much as it is storage, and a larger, better-finished block is a real, tangible thing that costs more to produce and ship than a compact 3-piece set's countertop stand or gift box.

What a bigger block does not buy is a sharper edge, a harder steel, or a more durable handle. It is storage and presentation, and the material and construction of the block itself is worth evaluating on its own terms, separately from the knives inside it. If the appeal of this set is specifically the look and scale of the block on your counter, that is a legitimate reason to pay more — it is a piece of furniture you will see every day. If the appeal is supposed to be the knives being sharper or better-made because the block is bigger, that reasoning doesn't hold; block size and blade quality are not the same claim, even when they're sold in the same box.

The math

The per-knife cost against the $199 sets in this catalogue

At $569.99 for 20 pieces, this set works out to roughly $28.50 per piece before accounting for the fact that several of those twenty pieces are typically steak knives, guards or a sharpening slot rather than distinct knife shapes. The imarku 14-piece Japanese gyutou set we reviewed separately runs $169.99, or roughly $12.14 per piece, and it publishes a specific 15-degree edge angle and a 56 ± 2 Rockwell figure that this listing does not. Put plainly: you are paying more than double per piece for this set, for a listing that gives you fewer checkable specifications, not more.

The comparison holds even against sets with an itemized, forged pedigree. The Martha Stewart Lockton 16-piece set states forged, full-tang construction at $84.99 — about $5.31 per piece — and still lands nowhere near this set's price per knife. The only way this set's math closes is if the six extra pieces over a 14-piece set, and the larger block, are themselves worth roughly $400 to you specifically. For some buyers furnishing a kitchen where the block is as much a design object as a tool, that case can be made. For a buyer comparing purely on knife quality per dollar, the $199-and-under tier of this catalogue does more with a published spec sheet than this set does with triple the price.

The ceiling

Where the price stops buying anything functional

Past a certain point in this category, additional money buys presentation and piece count rather than a functionally sharper or more durable knife. A chef's knife cuts a tomato the same way whether it costs $30 or $300 once it's properly sharp; what changes with price is typically the steel's edge retention, the block's finish, and how many pieces are bundled in. This set's listing does not give us the specific figures — an edge angle, a Rockwell hardness number — that would let us say definitively where, inside its own $570, the functional improvements stop and the presentation premium begins.

What we can say, by comparison with the rest of this catalogue, is that the published, checkable specifications stop well before the price does. imarku's $169.99 set states a specific edge angle and hardness figure; this $569.99 set, in the data we have on file for this exact ASIN, states a piece count, a block, and two marketing adjectives. If a published spec sheet is what you're paying for, you get more of one from the $170 set than from this one. If what you're paying for at $570 is a specific number of pieces and a larger, nicer-looking block, that's a legitimate thing to buy — but it's a different purchase than buying a measurably sharper or harder knife, and the price tag alone doesn't tell you which one you're getting.
