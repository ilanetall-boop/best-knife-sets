The geometry

The 15° edge, and how that compares with a German-style set

imarku's listing states its blades are hand-sharpened to a precise 15-degree angle per side. That's a specific figure, and it sits squarely inside the range Japanese-style gyutous are typically ground to, which runs narrower than the roughly 20 degrees per side common on Western, German-style double-bevel knives. A thinner edge angle means less steel meeting the food at the cutting line, which in practice means less downward force is needed to start a cut — the blade falls through a tomato skin or a stack of herbs rather than needing to be pushed through it.

The trade is the same one that applies to any narrow bevel, regardless of brand: less steel supporting the edge also means less tolerance for lateral stress. Twisting the blade, prying something apart, or cutting through bone or frozen food puts exactly the kind of sideways load on the edge that a 15-degree bevel handles worse than a thicker, more obtuse German-style grind. As a matter of blade geometry, not brand: a wider edge angle is the more forgiving choice around bones and frozen packaging, and a narrower one like this is the more precise choice for produce, fish and boneless cuts, provided it's used and cared for accordingly. For more on how stainless grades and heat treatment interact with edge angle, see our explainer on what "high carbon stainless steel" actually guarantees.

imarku pairs that edge angle with a second published figure: a Rockwell hardness of 56 ± 2 HRC, reached through repeated forging and low-temperature quenching. Mid-fifties Rockwell is a sensible, common range for a kitchen knife meant to be used daily — hard enough to hold the 15-degree edge for a reasonable stretch between touch-ups, soft enough to bring back without specialist equipment when it does dull. Publishing a specific hardness figure, rather than leaving it at "premium steel," is itself worth noting; it's a number a buyer could in principle check, which is more than most listings offer.

Upkeep

The built-in sharpener: does it keep up with daily use

The wooden block includes a built-in sharpener, and imarku's own framing for it is squarely aimed at beginners: draw the blade through the slot a few times and it restores what the listing calls the factory-sharp edge, with no separate stone, no separate tool, and no technique to learn. That's a real, specific feature, not a vague "easy to maintain" claim, and it addresses a genuine gap: most people who own a knife block never sharpen anything in it, and a slot that touches up the edge every time the knife goes back in the block is the single most effective way to keep a set usable for someone who will never own a whetstone.

What a built-in, pull-through sharpener cannot do is reset a damaged edge. It removes metal in a fixed, predetermined way, at a fixed angle, which is well suited to light, routine maintenance and poorly suited to fixing a chip or a badly rolled edge from a bad cutting-board habit or an accidental drop. For that, a whetstone or a professional sharpening service is still the right tool. It's also worth being careful about the angle match: a sharpener built for this block is presumably set close to the 15-degree edge the knives ship with, but a generic pull-through sharpener bought separately for a Western-style 20-degree edge would grind away more steel than necessary on a blade this narrow, gradually changing the geometry you bought the knife for. For a closer look at how slot sharpeners compare with honing steels, see our guide to pull-through sharpeners versus honing steels.

Whether it keeps up with daily use depends on what that means. For routine touch-ups between proper sharpenings, a built-in slot sharpener used every time the knife is put away is a reasonable, low-effort habit that should keep a working edge working. For a kitchen that uses these knives hard every day — heavy prep, bone-in cuts, careless storage — no pull-through sharpener is a substitute for an occasional pass on a whetstone.

That's confirmed, and it matters: a full-tang knife of this size should feel evenly weighted rather than blade-heavy or handle-heavy, and a full tang is one of the two or three construction details actually worth checking before buying any knife set.

The decision

Who should buy this instead of a German 15-piece block

A German-style 15-piece block set, stamped or forged, is typically ground nearer 20 degrees per side, built for a wider range of jobs including bone-in cuts and frozen packaging, and sold on piece count. This imarku set makes the opposite trade: fewer named extras, one clearly specified edge geometry, and a sharpening mechanism built around maintaining that specific angle.

Choose this set over a German-style 15-piece block if the narrower edge is something you want on purpose: if most of your cutting is produce, fish, boneless meat and herbs rather than bone-in roasts or frozen blocks, and if you'd rather draw a knife through a built-in slot than own and learn a whetstone. Choose a German-style set instead if your kitchen regularly needs a tougher edge near bone or ice, if you want an itemized contents list spelled out knife by knife the way some comparable block sets in this catalogue publish, or if an explicit dishwasher-safe or non-slip-handle claim is a requirement rather than a nice-to-have. The two are built for different habits, not different quality tiers, and the honest version of this decision is about which habit matches your kitchen, not which set is objectively better.
