Most of a 15-piece block is filler.

Before you buy

Four things decide this purchase. The piece count is not one of them.

Count the knives you will actually use

A “15-piece” set is typically eight knives, six steak knives and a block — or seven knives, a sharpener, shears and a block. Cuisinart publishes its contents piece by piece, which is the behaviour to reward. Before comparing two sets on their headline number, read both lists. You are usually choosing between the same three useful knives and different amounts of packaging.

Stamped or forged

A forged blade is hammered from a single bar: heavier, usually with a bolster where blade meets handle, and more expensive. A stamped blade is cut from sheet steel: lighter, thinner, cheaper, and sharp. Neither is simply better — forged suits people who like weight in the hand, stamped suits people who find heavy knives tiring. Most sets under two hundred dollars are stamped, and that is fine.

The sharpener in the block

Several sets build a sharpener into the slot, so the knife is drawn through it every time you put it away. imarku and Ninja both do this. It is a genuine convenience for people who would otherwise never sharpen anything, and a crude tool compared with a whetstone. If you already own a stone, the built-in sharpener is not a reason to pay more. If you do not, it is the single feature most likely to keep the set usable.

Hardness, and what the number means

Rockwell hardness — imarku states 56 ± 2, Brewin states 56+ — describes how hard the steel is. Harder steel holds an edge longer and chips more easily; softer steel dulls sooner and is easier to bring back. Mid-fifties is the sensible range for a kitchen set that will meet bones, frozen edges and a careless dishwasher. A published figure is also a sign the manufacturer expects to be checked.

What we didn’t

We do not physically test these knives, we do not own them, and we do not publish invented scores out of ten. Edge retention is measured on machines in laboratories, not by a writer chopping onions for an afternoon, and anyone presenting the second as the first is inviting you to trust a feeling. What we can do honestly is read every specification, set them against each other, and say which claims are checkable and which are adjectives.

FAQ

Common questions

How many knives do I actually need?

Three, and the third is arguable. A chef’s knife of 8 inches does nearly everything. A small paring knife handles what the chef’s knife is too big for. A serrated bread knife does the one job neither can, because a straight edge crushes a loaf rather than cutting it. Everything else in a block — the slicer, the santoku, the bird’s beak, the six steak knives — is either a duplicate or an occasion. That does not make a block set a bad buy; it makes the piece count a bad way to compare two of them.

Stamped or forged — does it matter?

It changes how the knife feels, not how well it cuts. A forged blade is hammered from one bar of steel, usually has a bolster where the blade meets the handle, and is noticeably heavier. A stamped blade is cut from a sheet: lighter, thinner, cheaper to make. People who like a knife to feel substantial prefer forged; people who do a lot of prep often find stamped less tiring. Cuisinart’s set here is forged at eighty-five dollars, which is unusual; most sets under two hundred are stamped, and they are not worse for it.

Is a built-in sharpener any good?

It is good at the thing most people will otherwise never do. A sharpener in the block touches up the edge every time you put the knife away, which keeps a working knife working. It is not equivalent to a whetstone — it removes material in a fixed way and cannot reset a damaged edge or change the angle. If you already sharpen your knives properly, it adds nothing. If you have never sharpened a knife in your life, it is probably the most valuable feature on this page.

Can I put them in the dishwasher?

You can, and it is the fastest way to ruin a set. Detergent is abrasive and alkaline, the heat is hard on handles, and knives knock against other items on the rack and roll their edges. High-carbon stainless resists rust but is not immune. Hand wash, dry immediately, and store in the block rather than loose in a drawer where the edges meet everything else. Every set here will last years longer for about ten seconds of effort per knife.

What does a Rockwell number tell me?

How hard the steel is, and therefore the trade it makes. imarku publishes 56 ± 2 and Brewin 56+; that mid-fifties range is where most good kitchen knives sit. Harder steel holds its edge longer but chips more readily and is harder to sharpen back. Softer steel dulls faster but forgives a bone or a frozen edge and comes back quickly on a stone. For a set that will be used by several people and occasionally mistreated, mid-fifties is the sensible place to be — and a manufacturer who publishes the figure at all is one expecting to be checked.
