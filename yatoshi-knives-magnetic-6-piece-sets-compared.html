Option one

The G10 handle, paired with a walnut block

G10 is a laminated fiberglass composite built by layering woven glass cloth with epoxy resin under heat and pressure, and it has been a standard handle material on working knives for decades precisely because it is not wood — it does not have grain, it does not absorb water, and it cannot swell, crack or warp the way a natural material can. Yatoshi's listing calls it a “textured fiberglass composite” and pairs it with the black-and-gold color option, finished with the concealed-tang construction described above. The texture is molded into the material itself rather than carved or sanded, which is the usual way G10 handles stay grippy when wet without needing a raised pattern cut into the surface afterward.

On grip, G10's main advantage over a polished wood handle is consistency: the same molded texture is there in a dry kitchen in January and a humid one in July, because the material itself doesn't respond to moisture the way wood does. On maintenance, that same inertness is the whole case for it — a composite handle doesn't need periodic oiling, doesn't need to be kept out of standing water for its own sake, and won't develop the fine surface checking that dry wood sometimes does over years. Yatoshi's own listing describes the result as “low-maintenance,” which is a fair characterization of the material in general, even though it's a marketing adjective rather than a figure we can independently check. On look, G10 reads as deliberately modern and workshop-grade: flat black with the gold accenting named in the listing's color field, no grain, no two handles subtly different from each other the way a natural material produces.

The block that ships with this set is walnut — a dense, tight-grained hardwood commonly used in knife blocks and cutting boards for its dark, even color and its resistance to the day-to-day dings a block takes on a countertop. Yatoshi describes it as double-sided and magnetic, with an anti-skid pad built in, matching the description given for the acacia block on the olive-wood set. The pairing itself is a small but deliberate styling choice: a composite black handle set against a dark walnut block, rather than against the lighter wood the other listing uses.

Option two

The olive wood handle, paired with an acacia block

The second listing swaps the G10 handle for one made from olive wood, keeping the same concealed-tang construction underneath. Olive wood is a dense hardwood long used for kitchenware and knife handles, prized mainly for how it looks: a warm, honey-to-brown base color running through with darker swirling grain lines, and because it's a natural material, no two handles carry exactly the same pattern. Yatoshi's listing names the color simply as “Olive” and, like the G10 set, describes the handle as low-maintenance and built for a secure grip — again, a characterization we can't independently verify, though it's consistent with how dense hardwood handles generally behave in a kitchen.

On grip, a wood handle like olive doesn't rely on a molded texture; it relies on the handle's shape and the natural slight tooth of the wood grain itself, and most owners of wood-handled knives find that grip perfectly secure as long as the handle is dry. On maintenance, that's the real trade-off against G10: wood handles, olive included, generally do better hand-washed and dried immediately rather than left wet in a sink, and most woodworking and cutlery guidance recommends an occasional light oiling to keep a wood handle from drying out over years of washing — a step a composite handle simply doesn't need. On look, this is the handle to choose if the warm, grain-forward aesthetic of real wood is part of why you want a set like this in the first place; it's a different visual language from G10's flat, modern black entirely.

The block paired with this handle is acacia wood rather than walnut — another dense hardwood, generally lighter in tone than walnut and known for prominent, often two-toned grain, commonly used in cutting boards and knife blocks for the same reasons: durability on a countertop and a warm, natural look. Like the walnut block, Yatoshi describes it as double-sided, magnetic, and fitted with an anti-skid pad. The styling logic here mirrors the G10 set in reverse: a natural wood handle set against a natural wood block, both in warmer tones than the composite-and-walnut pairing.

Under the handle

What doesn't change, no matter which handle you pick

Strip the handle and the block out of the comparison and what's left is, on paper, one knife sold twice. Both listings state a VG-10 cutting core — a Japanese stainless steel widely used in higher-end kitchen cutlery for its ability to take and hold a fine edge — wrapped in 67 layers of stainless-steel Damascus cladding. The cladding's job is mostly structural and visual rather than functional: it's softer, more flexible steel folded around the harder VG-10 core, which protects that core from chipping on side impact and produces the wavy Damascus pattern visible on the blade face. Both listings state the same layer count, so neither handle option is paired with a different grade of cladding or a thinner wrap.

Both also state a Rockwell hardness of 60-61 for the core. That number sits toward the harder end of what's common in kitchen cutlery — harder than the mid-fifties range typical of many Western-style stamped knives, which generally trades some edge retention for easier sharpening and a bit more forgiveness against a bone or a frozen edge. A core in the 60-61 range should, in principle, hold a sharpened edge longer between touch-ups, in exchange for being less forgiving of the kind of lateral stress a thin edge takes when it meets something hard it shouldn't. That trade-off applies equally to both sets, since the stated hardness doesn't change with the handle.

And both state the same edge geometry: a Japanese double bevel ground to 16 degrees on each side, described on both listings as meticulously hand-shaped. A double bevel means the edge is ground symmetrically on both faces of the blade, as opposed to a single-bevel edge ground almost entirely on one side, which is more common on traditional single-edge Japanese blades like a yanagiba. Sixteen degrees per side is a narrow angle by Western standards — many German-style kitchen knives sit closer to 20 degrees per side — and a narrower angle generally means a finer, sharper-feeling edge that's also less forgiving of hard contact than a wider one. That geometry is the one part of either knife that no handle choice touches at all; it's ground into the steel itself before either handle is ever attached.

The actual decision

The pick, by which material survives your sink habits

Because the blade, the price and the block mechanism are identical, this comparison reduces to one honest question: how do you actually treat a knife handle day to day? If knives in your kitchen routinely sit in a sink full of water for a while before they get washed, or your kitchen runs humid, or you'd rather not think about oiling a handle on any kind of schedule, the G10 set is the more forgiving choice. A fiberglass composite handle doesn't care how long it sits wet, because there's no grain for water to work into and nothing to dry out over time the way a natural material can.

If you hand-wash and dry your knives promptly as a matter of habit, and the warm, grain-forward look of real wood is part of why you're drawn to a set like this at all, the olive wood handle gives you that look without asking for much in return — just the ordinary care any wood-handled knife benefits from, and an occasional light oiling if you want to keep the finish looking fresh for years rather than just months. Neither choice is the objectively better knife, because under the handle there isn't a knife to choose between — VG-10 core, 67-layer cladding, 60-61 Rockwell and a 16-degree double bevel are the same spec sheet twice. This is a decision about maintenance habits and countertop aesthetics, priced by Yatoshi as if that decision were worth exactly nothing either way.

FAQ

Common questions

Is one Yatoshi six-piece set more expensive than the other?

No. Both the G10-handle set and the olive wood-handle set are listed at $189.99. Yatoshi doesn't charge a premium for either handle material over the other, which is unusual — wood handles and composite handles often carry different prices on comparable sets from other brands.

What's the actual difference between the two sets?

The handle material and the wood of the magnetic block it ships with. One set has a G10 fiberglass composite handle in black and gold with a walnut block; the other has an olive wood handle with an acacia block. Both listings state the identical VG-10 core, 67-layer Damascus cladding, 60-61 Rockwell hardness and 16-degree double bevel per side.

Is G10 or olive wood the better choice if I'm not careful about drying knives right away?

G10 is the more forgiving material if knives in your kitchen sometimes sit wet for a while before washing, since it's a fiberglass composite that doesn't absorb moisture or need periodic oiling the way a natural wood handle generally does. Olive wood looks warmer and more grain-forward, but it rewards being hand-washed and dried promptly, like most wood-handled kitchen knives.

What does "concealed-tang design" mean on these listings?

The tang is the part of the blade's steel that extends back into the handle. A concealed-tang design hides that steel inside the handle material rather than leaving it visible as a full-tang strip with through-rivets along the top and bottom edge. Both Yatoshi listings use this construction, so it's identical on the G10 set and the olive wood set — it isn't something that changes with the handle choice.
