Buying Guide · Japanese-Style Knife Sets

A Few Degrees Change Everything

A gyutou is the Japanese answer to a chef's knife, and the difference that actually matters is not the name — it's the angle. Where a German-style blade is typically ground nearer 20 degrees per side, a Japanese-style edge is usually finished narrower, often closer to 15. That thinner geometry bites into food with less pressure and holds a finer line, and it is also less forgiving of a twist, a bone, or a trip through the dishwasher. The vocabulary

Gyutou, santoku, and what the words actually mean

Gyutou translates roughly to "beef knife," and despite the name it is the Japanese equivalent of a Western chef's knife — a long, slightly curved, double-bevel blade meant to do most of the cutting board's work: slicing, chopping, mincing, breaking down a chicken. The curve near the tip is usually gentler than a German chef's knife, which changes the cutting motion slightly toward a push-and-draw stroke rather than a full rock. Two of the three sets in this guide use "gyutou" directly in their listing titles, which is the clearest signal a manufacturer can give that the blade profile follows that Japanese convention rather than a European one.

Santoku means "three virtues," a reference to the three jobs it is traditionally built to handle well: slicing, dicing and mincing. The blade is shorter and flatter than a gyutou, with far less curve toward the tip, which trades the rocking motion of a chef's knife for a straighter push cut — useful on a cutting board, less useful if you like to rock through a pile of herbs. It's worth knowing the term because it shows up constantly in searches for this category, but none of the three sets below are sold as a santoku; all three are built around a gyutou-style or general chef's-knife profile, so we won't stretch the santoku name onto a blade that isn't marketed as one. The geometry

15 degrees against 20: what a narrower edge changes

Every double-bevel knife is ground at some angle on each side of the blade, and that angle is the single biggest factor in how a knife feels in use. Western, German-style blades are conventionally finished nearer 20 degrees per side — a thicker, more obtuse edge that trades some bite for durability. Japanese-style edges are typically ground narrower, often closer to 15 degrees per side, which is a meaningfully thinner wedge of steel meeting the food. The imarku set with ASIN B0G5GGC7FP states this directly in its own listing, describing an "ultra-sharp 10–15° edge" on its gyutou blades — a figure we're repeating because the manufacturer published it, not because we measured it ourselves.

What that narrower angle changes in practice: less force is needed to start a cut, because a thinner wedge separates fibers with less resistance. On a tomato, a fillet of fish, or a stack of herbs, the difference is obvious the first time you feel a blade fall through with almost no downward push. The trade is durability at the very edge. A 20-degree bevel has more steel supporting the cutting line, so it tolerates a bit of lateral stress — a twist, a scrape against a bone — before it deforms. A 15-degree bevel has less steel there, so the same lateral stress is more likely to roll or chip the edge rather than just dull it. Neither angle is objectively better; they're suited to different habits, and the next two sections are about exactly where that trade pays off and where it doesn't.

The steel

Hardness and chipping: the trade you're accepting

Edge angle is only half the story; the other half is how hard the steel itself is, usually expressed as a Rockwell hardness number. Harder steel holds a fine edge longer between sharpenings, because the metal resists the slow rolling and flattening that dulls a blade over weeks of normal use. The cost of that hardness is brittleness: harder steel is also more prone to chipping at the very edge when it meets something unexpectedly hard or gets twisted mid-cut, and a chip in a narrow 15-degree edge is both more likely to happen and more noticeable than the same impact on a thicker, softer 20-degree edge. That's the real trade a Japanese-style set asks you to accept — a finer edge that cuts more easily and needs a bit more care to keep that way.

None of the three sets in this guide publish a specific Rockwell figure in their current listings, so we won't attach a number to any of them that the manufacturer hasn't stated — the same rule we apply to every spec on this site. What all three do share, by virtue of being high-carbon stainless kitchen knives, is the general shape of that trade-off described above. The honest limit

What a Japanese-style edge does not suit

A narrower edge is a specialist's tool, and being honest about its limits matters more than listing its strengths. It is a poor choice for cutting through bone or frozen food, where the blade meets hard, unyielding material head-on rather than slicing through soft fibers; that kind of contact is exactly the lateral stress a thin bevel tolerates worst, and it's how a fine edge chips rather than simply dulls. It's also a poor choice for anyone who twists the blade while cutting — prying apart a stuck lid, levering a stubborn squash in half, or using the tip as a makeshift screwdriver. None of that is specific to any one set here; it's true of the geometry itself, regardless of brand or price.

It's also worth being plain about what we can and can't say for each set in this guide. The imarku B0G5GGC7FP listing states its handles are ergonomic, non-slip and dishwasher safe; we have no dishwasher-safe claim on file for the other two sets reviewed below, so we're not extending that claim to them. A dishwasher is hard on any edge regardless of what the listing says — detergent is abrasive, the heat stresses handles, and knives knocking against other items in the rack roll edges that a narrower Japanese-style grind can least afford to lose. Hand washing and drying immediately remains the safer habit for any of the three sets below, dishwasher-safe claim or not. And if your kitchen regularly needs a knife for bone-in cuts or frozen packages, keeping one separate, sturdier blade for that job — rather than reaching for the gyutou — is the simplest way to keep a fine edge fine. Upkeep

Caring for a finer edge without a whetstone

A whetstone is the proper tool for resetting a narrow edge, but it has a learning curve, and plenty of people who'd benefit from a Japanese-style grind will never own one. There are still several habits that keep a 15-degree edge usable without a stone in the house. A honing rod — ceramic rather than the ridged steel rods bundled with some Western sets — realigns a slightly rolled edge between uses rather than removing metal, which suits a thinner bevel better than an aggressive steel rod would. Drawing the blade through at a shallow, consistent angle a few times before you start cutting is often enough to bring back the bite you felt when the knife was new.

A built-in sharpener, like the one in the imarku B0G5GGC7FP block, is a real convenience for exactly this problem: drawing the knife out of its slot touches up the edge automatically, with no separate tool to buy or learn. It is a blunt instrument compared with a stone — it removes material in a fixed way and can't fully recover a chipped or badly rolled edge — but for routine upkeep on a narrower edge, it's genuinely useful. Be cautious with generic pull-through V-notch sharpeners sold separately for Western knives; many are set to a wider angle than a Japanese-style edge is ground to, and pulling a 15-degree blade through a 20-degree slot grinds away more steel than necessary and gradually changes the edge geometry you bought the knife for.

Beyond sharpening, the basics matter more on a finer edge, not less. Cut on wood or soft plastic, never on glass, stone or a plate — those surfaces are harder than the steel and dull a narrow bevel within a handful of strokes. Hand wash and dry immediately rather than letting a blade sit wet in a sink, where it knocks against other dishes and rolls its edge. Store it in the block, a sheath or a blade guard rather than loose in a drawer, where the edge meets everything else sharing that space. None of this requires owning a stone; it requires treating a narrower edge as the more specialized tool it actually is.

FAQ

Common questions

What's the difference between a gyutou and a regular chef's knife?

They do the same job — slicing, chopping, mincing — but a gyutou is the Japanese version of that shape, typically with a gentler curve near the tip and, as a style, often finished at a narrower edge angle than a German-style chef's knife. The imarku B0G5GGC7FP states a 10 to 15 degree edge directly in its listing, compared with the roughly 20 degrees per side common on Western double-bevel blades. The practical result is a gyutou that bites more easily into food and asks for a bit more care around bones, twists and the dishwasher.

Is a narrower edge actually sharper?

It's thinner, which usually means it needs less force to start a cut, and that's often what people mean by "sharper" in everyday use. But sharpness also depends on how fine the final polish is and how well the edge is maintained, not the angle alone. A well-maintained 20-degree edge can out-cut a neglected 15-degree one. The angle changes how the knife feels and how it needs to be cared for; it doesn't guarantee a better result on its own.

Can these go in the dishwasher?

Only one of the three sets here, the imarku B0G5GGC7FP, states dishwasher-safe construction in its own listing; we have no such claim on file for the other two, so we don't extend it to them. Even where it's stated, we'd still recommend hand washing: detergent is abrasive, the heat stresses handles, and knives knocking against other items in the rack can roll an edge that a narrower Japanese-style grind can least afford to lose.

Do I need a whetstone to keep a Japanese-style edge sharp?

Not for routine upkeep. A ceramic honing rod realigns a slightly rolled edge between uses without removing metal, and a built-in sharpener like the one in the imarku B0G5GGC7FP block handles basic touch-ups automatically. A whetstone is still the right tool for fully resetting a damaged or badly dulled edge, but you can keep a finer edge usable for a long stretch without owning one — just be cautious with generic pull-through sharpeners built for wider Western angles.
