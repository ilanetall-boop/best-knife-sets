A Sharpener In the Block: Worth It?

Draw the knife out, draw it back, and a steel or ceramic slot touches the edge on the way. That is the entire mechanism behind a built-in sharpener, and it is exactly as useful, and exactly as limited, as that sentence suggests. It keeps a working edge working. It cannot rescue a damaged one, and it is not the same job as sharpening on a stone. We compared published specifications, current Amazon prices and the construction details of three sets that build a sharpener into the block, to work out who actually benefits.

What's actually happening

What does a built-in sharpener actually do to the edge?

Inside the slot sits a pair of abrasive wheels, crossed ceramic rods, or in a few designs a fixed carbide notch, set at a shallow angle on either side of where the blade passes through. Every time you slide the knife into its place in the block, the edge brushes against that abrasive for a fraction of a second, and a tiny amount of steel is removed from each side. Do that daily, and the edge is realigned and lightly refreshed before it has had the chance to go properly dull. Skip it for a month, and the edge drifts the way any working edge drifts — from use, from the cutting board, from the dishwasher if it ever ends up there.

The mechanism is passive and automatic, which is both the appeal and the ceiling. Nobody has to remember to do anything beyond putting the knife away, which is the one step in knife care that nobody skips. The imarku 14-Piece Japanese Gyutou set builds this into the block alongside its stated 15-degree-per-side edge, and the Ninja NeverDull 17-Piece set — the clue is in the name — is built around the same idea with an edge angle the brand describes as optimised for the mechanism. The McCook 15PC set states a built-in sharpener in its own listing title, the same basic premise at a different price point. In every case, the amount of steel removed per pass is small by design; it has to be, or the block would wear a knife down to nothing within a year.

What a fixed-angle slot cannot do is adapt to the knife. It sharpens at whatever angle the mechanism was built to, close to the angle the knife shipped with. Sharpen the same knife by hand at a different angle, and the two methods start fighting, each grinding against a slightly different edge than the one the other left behind.

Two different jobs

Honing against sharpening: two different jobs, often confused

Honing realigns an edge that has rolled slightly out of true from normal use — it does not remove meaningful steel, it just straightens what is already there, the way a honing steel does before you start cutting. Sharpening removes steel to create a new edge, because the old one has worn down or chipped past the point where realigning it does anything. A built-in sharpener sits closer to honing than to sharpening, even though manufacturers understandably call it a sharpener, because "touches up your edge" is a more sellable phrase than "delays the day you need a stone."

That distinction matters because of what it implies about frequency and expectation. A honing steel is meant to be used before nearly every cutting session and removes almost nothing. A whetstone is meant to be used every few weeks to months, depending on how hard the knife works, and removes a deliberate, visible amount of steel to form a fresh edge from scratch. A built-in sharpener lands in between: used constantly, like honing, but actually abrading the steel a little, like sharpening — just not nearly enough of it to reset an edge that has gone properly blunt. Ask it to take a genuinely dull knife and make it sharp again from a standing start, and the slot will keep doing its small pass with every draw while the knife keeps feeling about the same, because the mechanism was never sized for that job.

The honest audience

Who a built-in sharpener is actually for

It is for the person who will never otherwise sharpen a knife. That is not a criticism — it describes most home cooks, and it is a large enough group that three different manufacturers have built entire sets around solving exactly that problem. If buying a whetstone and setting aside fifteen minutes every couple of months sounds like something you will keep meaning to do and never actually do, a sharpener that works automatically every time you put the knife away is solving a real problem, not an imagined one. As the guide on how many knives you actually need gets into, most kitchens lean on two or three blades constantly, and keeping those few genuinely usable matters more than the size of the block they live in.

It is also for anyone sharing a kitchen with people who will not maintain a knife, however much the person who bought the set intends to: a block that sharpens on every put-away depends on nothing, which is precisely the point. It is a weaker case for someone who already owns a whetstone and knows how to use it. If that describes you, the mechanism is not doing nothing — it still realigns the edge between stone sessions — but it is not the reason to choose one set over another, and paying extra for it is paying for a feature you will barely notice using.

The limits

What a built-in sharpener cannot fix

It cannot repair a chipped edge. A chip is a small piece of steel missing from the blade, usually from hitting bone, a frozen surface, or the inside of a dishwasher rack, and fixing it means grinding the entire edge down past the depth of the chip — far more steel removal than a pass-through slot is built to do. Run a chipped knife through the block daily and the chip will still be there months later, lightly polished at the edges but structurally unchanged. Nor can it change the edge angle: the slot is set to one angle and will keep sharpening at that angle regardless of what the blade actually needs, so a knife reground steeper by a previous owner won't be corrected by a mechanism that only knows how to repeat itself.

It cannot rescue a blade that has gone through the dishwasher repeatedly, either. Detergent is abrasive and alkaline, and the heat and tumbling roll and dull an edge in ways that are about bulk wear, not a single damaged spot — generalized dulling a slot can slow but not reverse. None of this makes the mechanism a gimmick; it is a maintenance tool with a narrow job. Repair, regrinding and recovery need a different tool.

What you're paying for

How much of the price is the block

Across the three sets in this guide, the price spread makes the point on its own: $64.99 for the McCook 15-piece set, $169.99 for the imarku 14-Piece Japanese Gyutou set, and $370.00 for the Ninja NeverDull 17-Piece set. None of that difference comes from the sharpening mechanism alone being three or six times more capable — a built-in slot is a relatively simple piece of engineering wherever it appears. What changes between these prices is the steel, the construction behind the knives, the materials and fit of the block, and the brand doing the selling. Compare two sets purely on the strength of "it has a sharpener built in," and you are really comparing a feature that costs a small, fairly fixed amount to add, bolted onto knives and blocks that cost very different amounts to make.

That is worth separating out before buying on price alone, the same way the guide to kitchen knife sets under $100 argues for reading the construction details instead of the headline piece count. A $65 set with a built-in sharpener is not a budget version of a $370 set with the same feature; it is a different knife, at a different weight, with different steel, that happens to share one mechanism. The sharpener is the detail that gets the marketing attention because it is the differentiator from sets that do not have one. The knives underneath it are where most of the price actually lives. Imagine the same three sets with the sharpening slot removed entirely: the price gap between them would barely move, because the slot itself is a small, mass-producible part. The sharpener is the feature you notice daily. It is rarely the feature you are actually paying for.

When to go further

When to buy a whetstone instead

Buy a whetstone when a built-in sharpener has clearly stopped being enough — when the knife drags through a tomato skin instead of slicing it, when you can feel a chip catch under your thumbnail, or when the blade has been reground or damaged in a way the slot was never built to correct. A stone lets you choose the angle and remove exactly as much steel as the edge needs. It takes longer to learn and longer to use, but it is the tool for repair, not just upkeep.

A whetstone and a built-in sharpener are not mutually exclusive. Someone who sharpens properly every few months can still own a set with a built-in sharpener and let the slot handle the in-between realignment; the mechanism does not undo careful stone work, it just keeps doing its small pass regardless. The scenario to avoid is paying extra specifically for the mechanism, as with the Ninja NeverDull set here, when you already have the habit and the stone to make it redundant.

Buy a whetstone, too, if cost-per-knife matters more to you than convenience. A decent whetstone is typically a one-time purchase under $40 that works on every knife in the house, including ones bought years apart from different brands with different edge angles — a fixed-angle block sharpener only ever serves the knives built for its own slot. For a household with knives from multiple sets, a stone is the more flexible long-term tool. FAQ

Common questions

What does a built-in sharpener actually do to the blade?

It passes the edge across an abrasive wheel, ceramic rod or fixed notch at a set angle every time you slide the knife into the block, removing a small amount of steel and realigning the edge. Done daily, it keeps an edge that is already in reasonable shape from drifting dull. It is not sized to remove enough steel to reset an edge that has already gone properly blunt or chipped.

Is a built-in sharpener the same as honing?

Close, but not identical. Honing realigns an edge without removing meaningful steel, the way a honing steel does. A built-in sharpener does remove a small amount of steel on every pass, which puts it between honing and full sharpening — frequent like honing, but mildly abrasive like sharpening, without doing either job at full strength.

Can a built-in sharpener fix a chipped or damaged edge?

No. A chip means steel is missing from the blade, and fixing it means grinding the whole edge down past the depth of the chip, which is far more material removal than a pass-through slot is built to do. It also cannot change the edge angle or reverse general dulling from repeated dishwasher use. Those problems need a whetstone or professional sharpening.

When should I switch to a whetstone instead?

When the knife visibly struggles with something it used to cut cleanly, when you can feel a chip in the edge, or when you want to reset the angle rather than maintain the one the block assumes. A whetstone is also the better long-term choice for a household with knives from several different sets, since a fixed-angle block sharpener only serves the knives it was built for.
